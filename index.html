Abstract

Long-horizon mathematical reasoning requires an agent to maintain and expose the right semantic state across iterations. Left unmanaged, the result is proofs that are locally convincing yet globally incomplete: a key lemma unproved, an assumption unchecked, a citation unsupported. We present Research Math Agents (RMA), which treats proof development as work on a persistent set of typed proof obligations rather than as a conversation to be continued. A research store holds the proof, its open obligations and their dependencies, prior attempts, literature, and evaluations; the Research Context Orchestrator retrieves from it the records each locally scoped operation needs, compiles them into a bounded context, invokes the operation, and writes the result back, so RMA can identify, prioritize, and repair missing steps while preserving progress across rounds. Across four evaluations that fail in different ways, RMA obtains the highest observed scores among the compared systems: a 42.5% solve rate on the independently evaluated SOOHAK Challenge Hard set against 12.5% for its standalone Claude Opus 4.8 backbone; 8 of 10 correct on First Proof B1 and 8 of 10 passing on B2 under one blind expert panel grading all systems, at a cost comparable to ProofCouncil and UCLA Moonshot; and more Lean 4-verified proofs on Formal Conjectures than a NanoProof baseline whose search costs about 2.2× RMA’s realized spend per target. Holding the harness fixed and replacing the typed store and its retrieval with a summary memory costs 1.9 judge points on held-out problems and 17 of 80 kernel-verified targets, the controlled evidence for the design.

Method

Research-level proofs usually fail by omission: the text reads as finished, but a required part of the argument is still missing. Repairing such a gap needs more than another critique of the current draft. It needs the unresolved claim itself, what depends on it, what has already been tried, and which literature applies. RMA therefore treats proof development as a state-management problem and organizes it into three layers.

Persistent Research Store. Each problem has one store S=(Π,I,M,L,K,H,E)\mathcal{S}=(\Pi,\mathcal{I},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{K},\mathcal{H},\mathcal{E})S=(Π,I,M,L,K,H,E). It holds proof revisions, open and patched issues with their repair attempts and claim-dependency records, meeting records, literature notes, a concept glossary, strategic insights, and evaluations. Each issue records the affected claim, the defect type, the missing justification, and the repair needed, so later rounds act on it directly instead of re-deriving the diagnosis from the proof text.

Research Context Orchestrator. Four complementary critic analyses find issues: an LM gap critic, a deterministic structural and dependency checker, a semantic completeness judge, and a fidelity analysis that asks whether the reading of the problem is the intended one rather than one that trivializes it relative to its own citations. Open issues are ordered by severity (P0–P3), with dependency impact — the number of unresolved downstream claims resting on the affected claim — as the tie-break inside a severity level; the dependency count is computed deterministically from a claim-dependency graph rebuilt every round. For every call, the orchestrator compiles the operation’s instructions, its task, the current proof, the records linked to it, and the operation’s own recent outputs, then drops whole records in ascending component priority until the observation fits the budget BBB, so a dropped record never leaves a lemma statement cut mid-sentence.

Locally scoped operations. The critic, solver, literature researcher, meeting, reviser, and evaluator each receive only their compiled observation and return a typed artifact that passes a deterministic write-back gate. The solver repairs one issue at a time with anchored patches. Meetings and coordinated revision handle strategy-level changes that local repairs cannot reach. A patched issue is re-opened whenever the next round’s discovery pass still finds the gap, so a repair that did not hold returns to the queue.

Results

We evaluate on four benchmarks with complementary evaluators: third-party expert grading (SOOHAK), blind review by mathematicians (First Proof B1 and B2), Lean 4 kernel verification (Formal Conjectures), and an LLM-judge protocol (ResearchMath). The default backbone is Claude Opus 4.8 throughout.

Controlled evidence for the typed store

The contrast above replaces the whole system. To test the narrower claim — that the typed proof-obligation store and its record selection, not any competent persistent memory, drive the gain — we hold the harness fixed and change only how earlier rounds are remembered. Matched-harness memory (MH-Mem) keeps the same operations, prompts, tools, critic analyses, severity-and-dependency ranking, stopping tests, and best-proof selection, and replaces the typed store by a rolling natural-language summary plus embedding retrieval over an untyped archive. GIR+best gives Generic Iterative Revision RMA’s own best-proof selection rule, so the two pipelines differ only in orchestration. All configurations run on 30 held-out ResearchMath problems, disjoint from every problem used elsewhere, under the same $10-per-problem budget and the same three-model judge panel, three independent runs each.

Formal Conjectures (Lean 4)

For Formal Conjectures, the same loop produces Lean 4 proofs, and compiler errors are routed back as ordinary repair issues. Inside the benchmark’s research category we group variants of the same conjecture and sample 300 groups uniformly with seed 42 at a pinned repository revision, one declaration per group, with no AI-solvability filtering. The target’s proof body is replaced by sorry, no declaration from the benchmark source tree may be referenced beyond those the statement itself uses, and the same axiom audit applies. NanoProof — an open-source AlphaProof-style reproduction — runs on the same targets with the same toolchain, Mathlib snapshot, and audit.

The two allocate resources differently — a fixed simulation count against a monetary cap — so this compares realized cost rather than a matched allowance, and the systems also differ in backbone, training, and search, so it is a system comparison.

Cost, scaling, and ablations

Varying only the budget, RMA improves from 4 expert passes to 8 as realized spend grows from $153 to $1,850, with no further gain at $3,571. Varying only the number of rounds, the judge score rises from 4.5 at one round to 8.5 at six, with per-round gains falling from 1.2 to 0.4.

In the component ablations, the two mechanisms that govern state cause the largest drops from 8.5: reducing the persistent store to a stateless variant gives 2.6, and dumping the full state into context instead of orchestrating it gives 3.4. Removing the dedicated literature operation gives 4.2, and weakening any single remaining component gives 5.4–7.5. Generic Iterative Revision at the same budget scores 4.5, so exactly the three configurations that break persistent state, bounded context selection, or retrieved literature fall below a plain critique-and-revise loop. Along each graded axis, the score rises monotonically as the mechanism is strengthened:

AxisBasicImprovedFull
Cross-round memoryStateless: 2.6Last round only: 4.8Full persistence: 8.5
Context handlingDump entire state: 3.4Naive truncation: 5.7RMA orchestration: 8.5
Issue orderingFIFO: 5.4Severity only: 7.1Severity + dependency: 8.5

On the memory and context ladders the step from Improved to Full (3.7 and 2.8) is larger than the step from Basic to Improved (2.2 and 2.3): keeping only the last round, or truncating the full state to fit the budget, recovers less than half of the gap — what matters is which records reach each call, not only that some state is kept or that the context fits.

What a panel score means. The 0–10 score is the mean of a three-model judge panel (Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro), which includes RMA’s own backbone and is not reweighted for self-preference. To calibrate it, we drew 8 of the 30 held-out problems at random and gave RMA’s delivered proofs to two mathematicians working independently with the producing system hidden. Of the 16 absolute verdicts, 11 were complete — proofs from a configuration the panel scores around 8.5 are ones mathematicians mostly, but not uniformly, call complete. The decisive contrast does not rest on this scale alone: the kernel control above repeats it with no model scoring anything.
